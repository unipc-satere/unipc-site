South Africa · July 2026

South Africa Sends Its Indigenous Rooibos Plant to the International Space Station

Seeds from rooibos — a plant indigenous to South Africa's Cederberg region and used for generations by Khoisan communities — are being sent to the International Space Station this October in a mission organized with MaxIQ Space. It's the first such experiment involving an African species. The seeds will spend at least six weeks in orbit before returning to Earth around December or January, when students will compare their growth against a control group as part of a STEM education program.

Rooibos director Dawie de Villiers called the seeds "the first indigenous South African species… to go to space." Beyond the novelty, the mission puts a spotlight on a plant that has anchored Khoisan cultural and medicinal practice for centuries and is now a global export, sold in more than 50 countries.

The plant's commercial success has not always reached the Indigenous communities connected to its origins: researchers estimate that less than 7% of rooibos-growing land is currently controlled by Indigenous farmers, despite a 2019 industry benefit-sharing agreement.

Namibia · July 2026

Namibian Firm's $50M Fuel Station Deal Signals Growing Indigenous Ownership in African Energy

Nasan Energies, a Namibian company founded by 33-year-old entrepreneur Miguel Hamutenya, has completed the acquisition of 52 Engen- and Shell-branded fuel stations across Namibia in a deal valued at roughly US$50 million. The purchase — beating out more than 50 other bidders — makes Nasan Namibia's third-largest oil marketing company and one of Southern Africa's fastest-growing fuel retailers. All of the company's shareholders are under 33.

"This is about proving that indigenous African companies have the expertise, capital and vision to own and grow strategic national assets," Hamutenya said in a statement announcing the deal.

Sources   IOL Business Report → · Punch → · African Media Agency (July 13, 2026)

Malawi · Africa · June 2026

Scientists and Communities Turn to Indigenous Trees to Address Africa's Food Crisis

Two recent reports converge on a single point: restoring Africa's native, food-bearing trees — not just replanting forest cover generally — may be key to addressing malnutrition. A study published in PNAS Nexus by researcher Emilie Vansant and colleagues found that most of the continent's roughly $1 billion in pledged landscape-restoration funding goes toward fast-growing exotic species rather than native ones, even though over 90% of Malawi's population currently cannot afford a healthy diet. Working with 21 civil-society organizations across Malawi, the researchers co-produced an illustrated planting guide for indigenous edible trees such as wild loquat and Mbula plum.

A companion discussion, held during an African Forest Forum webinar, underscored both the opportunity and the risk: forest ecosystems rich in indigenous food species — like the 31 documented tree-food species in Benin's Upper Guinean Forest — could meaningfully diversify diets, but the traditional ecological knowledge needed to sustain them is eroding as younger generations move away from these practices.

Uganda · Educational resource

New Video Series Examines Women's Role in Western Uganda's Indigenous Dairy Traditions

Carleton University's Institute of African Studies has released an in-depth video exploring gender-based strategies and Indigenous knowledge systems in dairy-farming households in western Uganda, with particular attention to the roles women play in sustaining these traditions. It's a useful long-form resource for readers interested in the intersection of gender, agriculture, and Indigenous knowledge systems in East Africa.

Mexico · 2026

Mexico City's Indigenous Communities Keep Ancestral Traditions Alive, Even as the City Grows Costlier

Centuries after Spanish colonization, Indigenous communities in and around Mexico City — built atop the former Aztec capital of Tenochtitlan — continue to sustain their culture through language workshops, artisan markets, traditional dance, and community festivals. Mexico City's PILARES network, roughly 300 community centers offering free educational and cultural programming, has become a key gathering point for that work.

President Claudia Sheinbaum marked the anniversary of Tenochtitlan's founding by describing it not as "a dead past" but as "the living pulse that beats beneath our capital city" — a nod to the continued presence of Indigenous identity in daily life. That cultural resilience persists even as Indigenous families in the city face higher rates of poverty, housing instability, and displacement than the general population.

Colombia · May 2026

Colombia Hosts First Latin American and Caribbean Indigenous Tourism Summit

Representatives from more than 18 countries gathered in Popayán, Colombia this year for the First Latin American and Caribbean Indigenous Tourism Summit, closing with a joint Declaration of Indigenous Tourism that sets shared principles for the region. The summit's organizers framed Indigenous tourism as something broader than an economic sector — a practice rooted in culture, spirituality, and land stewardship, with Indigenous communities themselves driving decisions about what happens on their territory.

"This meeting is much more than a summit; it is proof that when peoples unite, they speak with one voice," said Felix Gonzalo Ojeda, general manager of Colombia's National Tourism Fund (Fontur). Colombia has also backed longer-term investments in this space, including an Intercultural Itinerant School of Indigenous Tourism established in 2023 on the Tacueyo reservation.

United States · June–July 2026

Princeton University Press Reading List Centers Indigenous Voices in America's 250th Anniversary Reflections

As part of a broader series marking the 250th anniversary of the United States, Princeton University Press has published a curated reading list centering Indigenous perspectives on the nation's history — from encounter and dispossession to reconciliation efforts underway today. Titles include Margaret D. Jacobs' After One Hundred Winters, which examines what reconciliation could mean given the country's history of dispossessing Indigenous Nations of their land.

It's a solid jumping-off point for readers — Indigenous and non-Indigenous alike — looking to deepen their understanding of this history, in the U.S. and, by extension, in Canada.

Source   Princeton University Press — "Reflecting on America's 250th Anniversary: First Peoples" →

Ontario, Canada · July 2026

Wildfires Devastate Namaygoosisagagun First Nation, Raising Questions About Emergency Response in Ontario

A fast-moving wildfire destroyed most of Namaygoosisagagun First Nation (also known as Collins First Nation) in northwestern Ontario in mid-July, forcing its roughly 130 registered members to flee by small boat with only minutes to gather belongings. Chief Helen Paavola has said the community received no advance warning from any government ministry before the fire arrived. Several other communities — including Whitesand First Nation, Kiashke Zaaging Anishinaabek, and Animbiigoo Zaagi'igan Anishinaabek — have also been placed under mandatory evacuation orders as fires continue to burn across the province.

The Assembly of First Nations has passed an emergency resolution calling on the federal government to provide immediate support and commit to rebuilding, while the Chiefs of Ontario and Anishinabek Nation have called for a public inquiry into the province's wildfire response. Indigenous Services Canada has said the reason Namaygoosisagagun did not receive an evacuation warning falls outside its department's scope. Anishinabek Nation is coordinating the community's rebuilding effort.
